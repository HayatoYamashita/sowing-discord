Sowing Discord

Exploiting the Lack of Key Commitment in E2EE Group RTC

One ciphertext, Two Views.

In an end-to-end encrypted Discord group call, a malicious participant can send a single encrypted media stream that one participant plays as one video and everyone else plays as another — and every client accepts it as cryptographically authenticated.

What is Discord?

Discord is one of the most widely used platforms for voice, video and text communication, with more than 200 million monthly active users. Since 2024, its DAVE protocol end-to-end encrypts audio and video in calls, so that only the participants hold the keys — the servers just forward encrypted media. Sowing Discord targets exactly these calls.

Overview

AES-GCM, the encryption used by Discord's end-to-end encrypted calls (DAVE), lacks key commitment: one ciphertext can be valid under two different keys. This was considered hard to exploit in live calls. We show that by combining a network adversary with a malicious participant, one ciphertext can be played as different audio or video by different participants — and both pass authentication.

  1. Phase 1

    Split the keys

    When someone joins or leaves the call, the key is updated. At that moment, a network adversary briefly delays the target's inbound TCP traffic, so the target stays on the old key while everyone else moves to the new key. Media keeps flowing, and the call does not drop.

  2. Phase 2

    Send one ambiguous ciphertext

    The malicious participant sends one ciphertext that decrypts under both keys, hiding the differences in parts the codec ignores. With H.265, it decodes into two different, clean videos. For short clips, generating it takes under a second.

Impact

What the attacker can do

  • Show one decision-maker a slide reading “Buy” while everyone else sees “Sell”.
  • Make only the target hear a short phrase such as “approve it”, while others hear brief noise.

What the attacker needs

  • To be a participant in the call.
  • To delay the target's network traffic (e.g., on the same Wi-Fi).

No server compromise, TLS decryption, or message tampering is required.

Ethical Considerations

  • We did not attack any production service or real user. All experiments ran in local, controlled environments using our own accounts and devices.
  • Before submitting the paper, we reported our findings, proof-of-concept implementations and proposed countermeasures to the affected vendors, giving them the opportunity to develop mitigations.

FAQ

What does this mean for users?

In an end-to-end encrypted Discord call, another participant may be able to make you see or hear something different from what everyone else sees or hears. Your app gives no warning, so neither you nor the others can tell that the views differ.

Who can carry out the attack?

Only someone who is already a participant in your call and can briefly delay your network traffic (e.g., on the same Wi-Fi). Outsiders cannot mount it, and it needs no server compromise or TLS decryption.

Did you report the vulnerability?

Yes. Before submitting the paper, we reported our findings, proof-of-concept implementations and proposed countermeasures to the affected vendors. Discord accepted the report as a valid vulnerability and awarded a bug bounty. See the disclosure timeline.

Did the vendors agree to this publication?

Yes. We coordinated the disclosure with the affected vendors and agreed with them on making this research public.

Did you attack real users?

No. All experiments ran in local, controlled environments using our own accounts and devices.

How can the attack be prevented?

Apps should bind the key ID to each frame by authenticating it, as SFrame (RFC 9605) does, and in the long term use an encryption scheme with key commitment. Either change makes a ciphertext valid under only one key.
